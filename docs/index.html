Which pages should an SEO team refresh first?

A ranking system that scores content for refresh review, validated on dates it has never seen, and benchmarked — honestly, including where it loses — against a hand-written rule.

ABSTRACT

Which content pages are most likely to recover — maintain or grow their 30-day impression volume — if an SEO specialist reviews and refreshes them this week? Using three months of anonymized page-level search and analytics data from FlyRank, we trained a Random Forest classifier on seven leakage-checked signals and validated it on a strict time-aware split: train on the first three weeks of March, test on the last ten days the model never saw. On that held-out window, the model's top-20 ranked pages hit Precision@20 = 0.90, against 0.65 for a hand-written CTR rule measured on the identical rows. The two systems agree on almost nothing — they target different populations entirely — so the shipped playbook routes disagreements to mandatory human review rather than picking a winner. This is decision-support for a weekly triage queue, not a causal or production claim.

Ziad Zakarya·FlyRank ML Internship·September 2026

The problem

An SEO specialist has capacity to review roughly 20–50 pages a week out of a catalog where thousands are eligible. Right now that queue is built by hand, or not built at all — pages get attention when someone happens to notice them declining, not on any consistent schedule.

The obvious fix is a rule: find pages with lots of visibility and zero clicks, flag them for a title-and-meta review. FlyRank already has one — it's the baseline this project measures itself against. The question this work asks is narrower and more useful than "build a model": does a learned ranking actually do better than the rule at the one thing that matters — the quality of the top 20 pages a specialist opens on a Monday morning?

This is decision-support, not automation. Every flagged page still goes to a human. What changes is which 20–50 pages that human sees first.

Windows

Feature context: Jan 1 – Mar 31, 2026 (90 days). Label: forward 30-day average impressions vs. the prior 30-day average, computed per page per decision date — no future date is ever used as a feature.

Excluded, and why

Pages with no Search Console instrumentation (gsc_data_available IS FALSE) carry no signal to score. Rows with fewer than 20 days of past or future coverage produce an unstable label. A gsc_avg_position of exactly 0 is a measurement placeholder, not a real top-of-page ranking — it's flagged, not treated as the best possible position.

A namespace mismatch worth naming

An earlier attempt to enrich this matrix with word-count and last-update metadata from FlyRank's published research dataset failed silently — a join that should have matched thousands of pages returned zero. Investigating showed the two releases use different anonymization pipelines: 0% of content IDs overlap between them. They are not the same page identifiers salted differently; they're two separate anonymized snapshots. Every feature in this system is therefore derived directly from the warehouse, never from the published dataset's metadata.

Methodology

Seven features, chosen from a larger candidate set and checked for leakage before anything was locked: gsc_clicks, gsc_avg_position, gsc_avg_position_is_placeholder, has_ga4_data, ga4_engaged_sessions, ga4_total_engagement_sec, sessions_organic. Two of these are instrumentation flags, not content signals — worth remembering when reading the results.

Label: binary. 1 if a page's forward 30-day average impressions are at least 90% of its prior 30-day average; 0 otherwise. Same-window, no lookahead.

Baseline: a hand-written rule — page-one position (1–10), impressions at or above the observed March 90th percentile (194), and zero clicks. Priority = raw impressions. Interpretable, defensible, and the bar the model has to clear.

Model: a Random Forest (300 trees, max depth 8). Logistic Regression was trained alongside it and included in the results below because it's an informative failure, not because it competes.

Three things worth sitting with

The base rate itself drifts. Recovery rate falls from 55.3% in the training weeks to 47.6% in the last ten days of March. Any fixed threshold is chasing a moving target — this is a monitoring requirement, not a footnote.

Precision@20 = 0.90 is a 20-row statistic. One flip moves it five points. Read it as a directional signal on one held-out window, not a stable, guaranteed benchmark.

The model and the rule aren't competing for the same pages. They share zero pages in their respective top-50 on any test date. The rule finds pages with visibility but no clicks — a CTR problem. The model finds pages with strong engagement already — worth protecting from decay. Different questions, both legitimate.

Limitations & honest framing

  • 1
    No causation. Refresh being associated with recovery does not mean refresh causes recovery. This is observational data — no A/B test, no difference-in-differences design.
  • 2
    One month of training data. The model has never seen anything outside Jan–Mar 2026. Predictions beyond a few months out are extrapolation, and the base-rate drift observed within March alone is a warning sign.
  • 3
    Time drift is material, not cosmetic. A 7.7-point base-rate swing inside a single quarter means any fixed operating threshold needs a monitoring plan, not a one-time calibration.
  • 4
    Two of the seven locked features are instrumentation flags, not content signals. If GA4 tracking coverage changes, model behavior changes with it — a drift exposure distinct from label leakage.
  • 5
    The namespace mismatch limits available signals. Word count and days-since-update live only in a dataset that cannot be joined to this one — the reason-code taxonomy is built entirely from warehouse-native signals.
  • 6
    Legal, medical, financial, and trademark content is excluded from automation regardless of score. The system flags; it does not dispose.
  • 7
    Two headline claims from FlyRank's own published research didn't survive re-audit. A "3.2× recovery boost" from refresh turned out to be a decay-slowing effect concentrated in low/mid-traffic pages that vanishes at the top. A word-count advantage for growing pages reversed sign in half the age buckets once content age was controlled for.

Ranked recommendations — the playbook that ships

Model output becomes a weekly review queue with a deterministic, human-readable reason attached to every row — never a bare score.

ACT_THIS_WEEK — 0
REVIEW_IF_CAPACITY — 0
Top 20/day, reserved capacity Ranks 21–50/day, if bandwidth allows

Reason codes (deterministic, priority order)

  • stale_visibleOld page, still earning traffic — refresh candidate
  • strong_engagementUsers engage deeply — protect from decay
  • high_position_trafficTop position, high visibility — expand opportunity
  • converting_visibilityRanking and converting — maintain
  • signal_unclearFallback — model flagged it, but the signals don't explain why
THE HEADLINE FINDING: DISJOINTNESS, NOT DISAGREEMENT

Measured on the ten test dates: the model's top-50 and the rule's top-50 share zero pages on any single date. Within the model's own queue, 40% of the top tier (200 of 500 rows, all in ACT_THIS_WEEK) are flagged as points where the two systems would have made an opposite call — routed to mandatory human adjudication rather than resolved automatically.

An earlier framing of this called it "80% disagreement" — a window-level count mistaken for a queue-level share. Restated with the correct denominator: 40% flagged, concentrated entirely in the top tier; zero overlap is the stronger and simpler fact.
